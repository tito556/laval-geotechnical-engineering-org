Seismic Tomography (Refraction & Reflection) for Geotechnical Projects in Laval

Laval’s growth from a patchwork of rural parishes on Île Jésus into Quebec’s third-largest city placed unique demands on its subsurface. The island’s geology, shaped by the Champlain Sea regression, left thick sequences of marine clay and till over fractured Ordovician limestone. When a new transit corridor or institutional building breaks ground in Chomedey or Sainte-Rose, guessing bedrock depth from sparse boreholes alone invites cost overruns. Our seismic tomography campaigns—both refraction and reflection—deliver continuous P-wave and S-wave velocity profiles that reveal the true rockhead topography, helping engineers size excavations and select foundation types with confidence. For deeper targets, we often pair a CPT test campaign with the tomographic model to calibrate soil stiffness where the velocity contrast between clay and till is subtle but mechanically critical.

Seismic tomography turns a few borehole data points into a continuous image of the subsurface, reducing the uncertainty that drives up contingency costs on Laval projects.

Methodology and scope

One recurring mistake we see on Île Jésus is assuming that a single borehole log captures the bedrock surface across an entire parcel. The limestone here can exhibit pinnacle-and-groove weathering, with elevation swings of 3 metres or more over a 20-metre distance. Refraction tomography with a 24- or 48-channel seismograph and a sledgehammer or weight-drop source maps these features as velocity isochrones, flagging zones where rock quality degrades below 2,500 m/s. Reflection processing, with careful CDP stacking, images sub-horizontal fractures and potential karst cavities before they become a problem during excavation. In the eastern sectors near the Rivière des Prairies, where sensitive clay layers are common, we incorporate a seismic refraction survey alongside electrical resistivity to distinguish intact clay from zones affected by post-glacial landslides, a detail that directly influences slope setback requirements under the Quebec Construction Code.

Local geotechnical context

Located at 45.5571° N, Laval lies within the Western Quebec Seismic Zone, where earthquakes of magnitude 5 or greater occur roughly every ten years. The M5.8 Montreal earthquake of 1732 inflicted serious damage on Île Jésus, and modern NBCC 2020 maps classify the region as site class C to E depending on soil thickness. To assign the correct site class and prevent overly conservative or unconservative spectral accelerations, seismic tomography yields the necessary Vs30 profile. Beyond seismic planning, Laval’s average elevation of 40 meters above sea level means that buried valleys filled with compressible Champlain Sea clay are ubiquitous. Missing a deep trough beneath a proposed high-rise in the Centre-Ville district could result in differential settlement and expensive underpinning. Tomographic cross-sections across these valleys enable the structural team to place deep foundations only where bedrock drops, rather than across the entire footprint.

Associated technical services

01

Refraction Microtremor (ReMi) and MASW Tomography

For NBCC site classification and liquefaction screening, combined active and passive surface-wave methods produce 1D and 2D shear-wave velocity profiles. These methods utilize linear arrays of 24 to 48 vertical geophones.

02

Crosshole and Downhole Seismic Tomography

Between cased boreholes, high-resolution P- and S-wave velocity measurements achieve layer resolution down to 0.5 meters. This is ideal for deriving modulus in layered till and rock for deep excavation shoring design.

03

Reflection Profiling for Bedrock Integrity

To image sub-horizontal fracture zones, karst voids, and weathered rock zones that refraction alone might miss—especially in the limestone formations beneath central Laval—common-depth-point stacking over spreads up to 120 meters is employed.

Regulatory framework

Relevant standards include ASTM D5777-18, NBCC 2020 (Division B, Part 4, Seismic Hazard), CSA A23.3-19 (concrete structures, seismic provisions), and BNQ 2501-092 (geotechnical site investigation, Quebec context).

Questions and answers

How much does a seismic tomography survey in Laval typically cost?

Depending on array length, source type, and whether both refraction and reflection are required, a typical survey on Île Jésus costs between CA$3,230 and CA$6,570. Projects requiring crosshole logging or very deep penetration may exceed this range; we provide a fixed-price proposal after reviewing site geometry.

What is the difference between refraction and reflection tomography?

Refraction tomography builds a subsurface velocity model from first-arrival times of seismic waves traveling along the soil-rock interface, working best when velocity increases with depth. Reflection tomography, on the other hand, stacks later-arriving energy to image interfaces where velocity may decrease—such as fracture zones or cavities—yielding a more detailed structural picture of the rock mass.

Can seismic tomography replace boreholes for site investigation in Laval?

Tomography complements boreholes rather than replacing them. It offers continuous lateral coverage between discrete drill points, reducing the number of boreholes needed. However, a few calibration boreholes remain essential for tying velocity values to specific lithologies and sampling clay for laboratory testing.

How long does a seismic tomography campaign take on a typical Laval site?

A single 115-meter refraction line with 24 geophones can be acquired in about two hours, with processed data delivered within three to five business days. Larger grids or reflection spreads require proportionally more field time, but most commercial lots in Laval can be covered in one or two field days.

Is seismic tomography feasible in winter conditions on Île Jésus?

Winter surveys are common in Laval. Frozen ground actually improves geophone coupling and reduces surface-wave noise, though the frozen surface layer must be accounted for in the velocity model. We use heated battery packs and low-temperature cabling rated to -30°C for reliable winter acquisition.
