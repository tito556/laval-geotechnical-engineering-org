Field Permeability Testing in Laval: Lefranc & Lugeon Solutions

The stainless-steel packer assembly arrives at the Laval jobsite looking deceptively simple for the data it delivers. A technician lowers the cylindrical tool down the drill casing to the test depth, inflates the rubber seals to isolate the zone, and begins the controlled water injection. In the limestone and shale that underlie much of Laval—where the Rivière des Prairies shapes the local water table—knowing the difference between a tight formation and one riddled with solution channels changes the entire project budget. The Lefranc test handles soil and highly fractured rock with a simple falling-head principle, while the Lugeon method applies stepped pressure stages to assess rock mass conductivity. Both provide the hydraulic conductivity values that drainage contractors and foundation engineers need before designing excavation support or permanent dewatering systems. For deeper investigations where stratigraphy is unclear, our team often runs a preliminary seismic refraction survey to map bedrock depth before positioning permeability test intervals.

A single Lugeon test in fractured limestone can reveal more about excavation risk than a dozen undisturbed samples tested in the lab.

Method and coverage

A mistake we see repeatedly in Laval is assuming a uniform permeability coefficient from desktop geology maps. The reality on the ground—especially near the old quarry zones in the eastern part of the island—is that a single borehole can pass through three distinct hydraulic regimes in fifteen meters. A contractor who skips field testing and relies on textbook values for glacial till will undersize their sump pumps and flood the excavation on day one. The Lefranc protocol captures point measurements in soil at the base of a borehole, using either constant-head or variable-head procedures depending on expected flow rates. The Lugeon test, developed for dam foundations, pumps water into an isolated rock interval at up to five pressure steps, generating a characteristic pressure-flow curve that reveals whether fractures are dilating, washing out, or sealing under load. This data feeds directly into steady-state seepage models and helps determine whether cut-off walls, grout curtains, or simple wellpoints are the right solution. The equipment is calibrated to ASTM D4630 for rock mass permeability, and our lab maintains ISO 17025 accreditation for the pressure transducer and flow meter systems used in the field.

Local geotechnical context

With an elevation of about 45 meters above sea level on Île Jésus, Laval's population is approaching 450,000, driving expansion into regions where till cover is thin and the underlying Ordovician limestone is hydraulically active. The 2010 Val-des-Bois earthquake, a magnitude 5.0 event, served as a reminder to engineers throughout the St. Lawrence lowlands that the Western Quebec Seismic Zone remains active. In dewatering scenarios, the primary daily concern is not seismic shaking but rather uncontrolled groundwater inflow through karst features. If a foundation pit intersects an open fracture network connected to the Rivière des Mille Îles, it can be filled faster than pumps can handle. Field permeability testing identifies these high-conductivity zones before excavation begins. The Lugeon value, expressed as the water loss in liters per minute per meter of test interval at one MPa of pressure, provides a direct and repeatable metric that geotechnical engineers use to determine whether grouting is necessary and the pressure at which the rock mass can accept grout without hydraulic fracturing.

Complementary services

01

Single-Zone Lefranc Testing

For commercial building sites in Laval, preliminary dewatering assessments often involve variable-head or constant-head permeability measurements in soil, weathered rock, or fill. The process includes borehole preparation, placement of a gravel pack, and falling-head data logging at intervals of 1 second.

02

Multi-Stage Lugeon Testing

A five-pressure-stage water injection test in competent rock employs a double packer assembly with a standard test interval of 3.0 meters. The test cycles through ascending and descending pressure steps to detect fracture dilation or clogging. This method is recommended for deep excavations near the Rivière des Prairies.

03

Integrated Permeability Profiling

When a vertical conductivity profile is needed, combined Lefranc and Lugeon testing can be performed down a single borehole with continuous packer repositioning. If fracture orientation is important for seepage modeling, televiewer logging is paired with the testing. This approach delivers a complete hydraulic cross-section for cut-off wall design.

Regulatory framework

The relevant standards include ASTM D4630-19, which specifies the Standard Test Method for Determining Transmissivity and Storage Coefficient of Low-Permeability Rocks by In Situ Measurements Using the Constant Head Injection Test; ASTM D5092/D5092M-16, the Standard Practice for Design and Installation of Groundwater Monitoring Wells; and ISO 17025, which outlines general requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories.

Questions and answers

How much does a field permeability test cost in Laval?

In Laval, the cost for a single Lefranc or Lugeon test typically falls between CA$830 and CA$1,490 per test interval, depending on depth and whether a single or double packer setup is needed. Mobilization, drill rig standby, and reporting are usually quoted separately. Multi-level testing programs offer more competitive per-interval pricing.

When should I choose a Lugeon test instead of a Lefranc test?

The Lugeon test is best suited for competent rock where assessing the hydraulic conductivity of discrete fracture systems under pressure is necessary. It is the appropriate choice for excavations in the limestone bedrock common across Laval. Conversely, the Lefranc test is more suitable for soil, heavily weathered rock, or backfill where the borehole wall cannot support packer inflation pressure without collapsing.

How long does a typical permeability test take on site?

A single Lugeon test with five pressure stages typically takes 60 to 90 minutes once the packer is set, while a Lefranc variable-head test in soil can be completed in 30 to 45 minutes. The most time-consuming variable is drill rig time: cleaning the borehole, advancing casing, and setting the packer at the correct depth add several hours per setup.

What data do I receive after the test?

The report you receive is signed and stamped, containing the test interval depths, raw pressure and flow rate data, calculated hydraulic conductivity (K in cm/s or m/s), Lugeon values where applicable, and pressure-flow interpretation plots. It also includes recommendations for dewatering flow rates and comments on whether grouting appears necessary.

Can these tests be combined with other site investigation work?

Yes, the same drill rig used for SPT sampling or rock coring can be used for packer testing on the same day. We coordinate with the drilling contractor to perform permeability tests immediately after the borehole reaches target depth and before grout or casing is installed, saving you a separate mobilization.
