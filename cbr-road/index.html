Laboratory CBR Testing for Pavement Design in Laval

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

LEARN MORE →

Designing a pavement structure in Laval-duvarie versus Fabreville reveals two completely different soil realities. The silty sands near the Rivière des Prairies in western Laval behave nothing like the sensitive Champlain Sea clays that dominate the central plateau around Chomedey and Pont-Viau. A laboratory CBR test becomes the only reliable way to quantify that difference before selecting subbase thicknesses. Our lab processes remolded specimens under controlled moisture and density conditions, following ASTM D1883-21 procedures, to determine the California Bearing Ratio that directly feeds into the AASHTO 1993 pavement design equation. For projects near the Sainte-Rose escarpment, where the till veneer is thin and variable, we often recommend pairing the in-situ permeability testing with the CBR to confirm drainage characteristics that affect long-term subgrade performance. Laval’s freeze-thaw cycles, averaging 80 per winter season, make accurate soaked CBR values non-negotiable for any road that has to survive beyond its first spring.

A soaked CBR difference of just 2 percentage points can change the required granular base thickness in Laval by over 150 mm.

Methodology and scope

In the Vimont industrial sector, we repeatedly observe that clay subgrades compacted at 95% of Standard Proctor maximum dry density still deliver soaked CBR values below 3%. That hard-won local observation changes everything about pavement design. The laboratory CBR test measures the penetration resistance of a 49.6 mm diameter piston into a compacted specimen, comparing it to a standard crushed stone reference. We run both unsoaked and 96-hour soaked specimens because Laval’s water table sits high across much of Île Jésus, often within 1.5 m of finished grade. The resulting CBR ratio dictates whether a project needs a granular subbase of 300 mm or 600 mm, directly affecting excavation volumes and material costs. For flexible pavement sections, the CBR value integrates with the flexible pavement structural number calculations specified by MTQ standards. When the subgrade CBR falls below 6%, we sometimes supplement the design with stone columns as ground improvement before placing the pavement layers, especially in the low-lying areas east of Highway 15.

Local geotechnical context

The pressing device moves at a rate of exactly 1.27 mm per minute, a speed that may appear extremely slow to observers but is essential for replicating the gradual deformation that road surfaces undergo under vehicle loads. In Laval, the moisture content of clay subgrade can vary by as much as 12 percentage points between August and April, making it crucial that laboratory CBR testing uses specimens with the correct molding water content; otherwise, the results will be misleadingly favorable. The primary technical challenge lies in sample preparation: field samples from the Sainte-Dorothée region frequently contain silt lenses that require careful homogenization to yield meaningful CBR values. Our specialists trim, compact, and soak each sample within a humidity-controlled environment kept at 23°C ± 2°C, recording swell measurements every 24 hours. If a specimen swells more than 5% during the soaking period, it indicates a subgrade prone to significant frost heave under winter conditions—a finding that supersedes the CBR value in design importance and is promptly noted in the geotechnical report.

Associated technical services

01

Soaked Laboratory CBR Test (ASTM D1883)

A standardized 96-hour soaking procedure is followed, with swell readings taken at each 24-hour interval. A three-point compaction curve is integrated to determine the CBR at target density and moisture levels. This approach is standard for Laval residential streets and commercial parking lots, where design is governed by spring thaw conditions.

02

Unsoaked CBR with Immediate Penetration

Penetration testing is carried out on samples at their as-compacted moisture content without any soaking. This method is appropriate for evaluating the support capacity of interior slab-on-grade floors in heated industrial buildings within Laval's industrial parks, where the subgrade does not become saturated.

Questions and answers

What is the typical turnaround time for a laboratory CBR test in Laval?

Standard turnaround time for reporting is five to seven business days from the date samples are received. The 96-hour soaking period is mandated by ASTM D1883 and cannot be reduced. For urgent projects, such as reinstating emergency utility trenches, an expedited unsoaked CBR test can be completed within 48 hours.

How much does a laboratory CBR test cost for a Laval project?

In Laval, the cost of a single-point laboratory CBR test ranges from CA$150 to CA$290, depending on whether soaking is required and the number of compaction points. A full three-point CBR curve with soaking falls near the upper end of this price range.

Which compaction standard should be used for the CBR specimen preparation?

The selection between Standard Proctor (ASTM D698) and Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557) depends on the pavement layer being considered. For residential streets in Laval, municipal specifications usually call for Standard Proctor at 95% of maximum dry density for subgrade and 98% for subbase. Modified Proctor is typically employed only for highway projects under MTQ jurisdiction.

Can CBR values from Laval clay subgrades be used directly for flexible pavement thickness design?

Yes, but there is an important local consideration. While the AASHTO 1993 design method relies on the CBR to calculate the structural number, Laval's Champlain Sea clays suffer significant strength loss under repeated freeze-thaw cycles. We advise applying a seasonal adjustment factor of 0.6 to 0.8 to the laboratory CBR when the water table lies within 1.5 meters of subgrade elevation—a frequent occurrence across much of central Laval.
