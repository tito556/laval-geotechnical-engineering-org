Exploratory Test Pit Services in Laval: Direct Subsurface Investigation for Geotechnical Projects

Much of Laval sits on the Champlain Sea clay plain, a post-glacial marine deposit that can extend over 30 meters deep in some areas near the Rivière des Mille-Îles. This sensitive silty clay, locally known as Leda clay, presents unique challenges when undisturbed samples are needed for laboratory strength testing. In our experience across Laval—from the commercial corridors of Chomedey to the industrial zones of Saint-Vincent-de-Paul—an exploratory test pit often provides the most reliable method for observing the transition between the weathered crust and the intact clay beneath. Unlike boreholes that retrieve small-diameter samples, a properly logged test pit allows the geotechnical engineer to walk the full profile, measure jointing in the underlying limestone bedrock where it outcrops, and collect large bulk samples for Proctor compaction testing when the project involves engineered fill placement. The water table in Laval is frequently encountered within 2 to 4 meters of the surface, and the pit walls in these saturated clays must be carefully evaluated for short-term stability before any technician enters the excavation.

Seeing the Chalmplain clay profile in a full-depth test pit reveals oxidation zones and shear planes that no borehole log can capture completely.

Methodology and scope

The freeze-thaw cycle in Laval, with winter lows regularly dipping below -20°C and spring thaws saturating the ground, creates a highly active zone in the upper 1.5 to 2.0 meters of soil. This seasonal movement can mask deeper stratigraphic issues if the investigation stops at shallow depths. An exploratory test pit in this region is typically advanced through the silty sand veneer left by the retreat of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, then into the underlying Champlain clay. We log the depth of oxidation, which in Laval often reaches 3 to 4 meters, and document the transition from stiff fissured clay to softer intact material. For sites in eastern Laval where granular deposits from the Saint Lawrence River paleo-channels appear, we combine the pit investigation with in-situ permeability testing to estimate hydraulic conductivity directly from the exposed face. Where limestone bedrock is encountered at shallow depth—common in the Pont-Viau area—the pit becomes a critical tool for assessing rock quality designation (RQD) and fracture spacing before designing shallow footings. The ASTM D2488 visual-manual procedure guides our field classification, and we routinely collect Shelby tube samples from the pit floor when the depth to competent material exceeds the safe limits of vertical open cuts.

Local geotechnical context

A common challenge in Laval is the erroneous assumption that the weathered crust constitutes a reliable bearing layer. The top 2 to 3 meters of Champlain clay often appear stiff and fissured, leading some to think shallow spread footings are adequate. In reality, the soft intact clay below can experience long-term consolidation settlement under load, endangering lightly reinforced structures. A pit that only reaches 2 meters may miss this critical change. Another concern is uncontrolled groundwater. In Laval's low areas near the Rivière des Prairies, the water table can quickly rise in spring, making pit walls that looked stable during excavation fail. We've seen near-vertical silty clay cuts slough unexpectedly after a few hours of air and moisture exposure. Proper shoring or benching per CNESST safety standards is required; no one may enter a pit over 1.2 meters deep without a competent person evaluating soil type and stability. Sensitive clay also demands care: vibrations from Autoroute 15 or Autoroute 440 traffic can remold saturated Leda clay, causing a local loss of shear strength to nearly zero.

Associated technical services

01

Visual Stratigraphic Logging and In-Situ Testing

Logging of each test pit is carried out by a geotechnical engineer or senior technician according to ASTM D2488. Parameters recorded include fill thickness, oxidized crust depth, intact Champlain clay consistency, and the presence of sand or silt partings that could serve as drainage paths. Pocket penetrometer and hand vane shear tests are conducted at regular intervals on pit walls to obtain preliminary undrained shear strength. If bedrock is reached, fracture orientation, infill material, and RQD are documented. Photographs of all four pit walls are included in the final report, with notes on sampling sites and water seepage points.

02

Disturbed and Undisturbed Sampling for Laboratory Analysis

Bulk disturbed samples are taken from each stratigraphic unit identified in the pit, stored in sealed containers for moisture content, grain size distribution, and Atterberg limits testing. For Champlain clay, thin-walled Shelby tubes are pushed from the pit floor to collect undisturbed specimens for oedometer consolidation and triaxial shear tests. Block samples are also taken when large specimens are needed for specialized analysis. All samples are shipped to our ISO 17025 accredited lab under chain-of-custody, with moisture retention measures maintained during transport.

Regulatory framework

ASTM D2488: Standard Practice for Description and Identification of Soils (Visual-Manual Procedure); CNESST Safety Code for the Construction Industry (Quebec) – Excavation and Trenching Provisions; ASTM D420: Standard Guide for Site Characterization for Engineering Design and Construction Purposes; CSA A23.3: Design of Concrete Structures (for foundation elements exposed in pit walls).

Questions and answers

What is the typical cost range for an exploratory test pit in Laval?

A standard exploratory test pit in Laval—including backhoe excavation to 3.5 meters, complete ASTM D2488 logging by a geotechnical engineer, bulk sampling, and backfill compaction—usually costs between CA$670 and CA$990 per pit. The exact amount depends on access conditions, whether hydrovac excavation is needed in congested utility corridors, and how many Shelby tube samples are taken from the pit floor. Projects with multiple pits on one site benefit from mobilization efficiency.

How deep can an exploratory test pit go in Laval's Champlain clay?

In the Champlain clay that underlies much of Laval, we typically limit vertical cuts to 4 meters in Type C soil per CNESST safety regulations. For deeper investigations, we bench the excavation or slope the sides at a ratio of 1.5:1. If the water table is encountered within the first 2 meters, the practical depth is often reduced due to seepage-induced instability. In those cases, we may combine the test pit with an adjacent SPT borehole to extend the investigation depth.

What information does a test pit provide that a borehole cannot?

A test pit provides a continuous, full-scale exposure of the soil profile, which a borehole cannot replicate. The geotechnical engineer can observe macro-structural features such as fissures, oxidation stains, shear planes, and root penetration that are invisible in split-spoon samples. It also allows for direct measurement of infiltration rates on exposed soil horizons and the collection of large, undisturbed block samples. For projects involving retaining wall design in Laval, seeing the actual backfill material and its layering is invaluable.

How are test pit locations selected for a site investigation in Laval?

Pit locations are chosen based on the proposed structure footprint, a desk study using historical maps of the Champlain Sea extent, and variability from earlier geotechnical reports in the area. In Laval, pits are often placed at building corners and one in the center to capture lateral changes in clay crust thickness. If the site is near a former watercourse or marsh—common in eastern sectors—those areas are targeted to detect organic deposits that widely spaced boreholes might miss.
